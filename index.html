Your attention is scored every day, by machinery running underneath every screen you touch. Below the glass, your attention is counted, weighed, and used to decide what happens to you next.

I spent twenty years above the glass designing what people see — commercials at Ridley Scott Associates, a cinematic VR studio, Lead Product Designer at Meta Reality Labs on a team called Engagement and Growth. Then I left to follow attention the whole way down: from the screens we touch, to the numbers that judge us, to the story you tell yourself about who you are.

The investigation

I’m writing a book about how we measure whether technology is good for people, and what those measurements miss as the machinery moves from screens into AI.

The other half of the book is about the opposite extreme: the scientists, teachers and traditions who train attention rather than capture it, and what they’ve learned that none of the machinery can see.

Over the next twelve months I’m interviewing the people who understand pieces of this better than I do — the researchers who build these instruments, the people who study them, and the people who train attention without any of them.

If you’re one of them, I’d like an hour.

The throughline

I came to this sideways.

I studied art history at Northwestern, then found film through post-production. Cubism had shown me the goal: an image that refuses a single fixed viewpoint. The Royal College of Art trained me as a designer; the pursuit carried on, bringing that eye to the moving image as a director at Ridley Scott Associates.

Virtual reality finally exploded the frame — not just past the screen but all the way around you, embodying what I’d spent years circling. I made cinematic VR and founded a studio to build it. Then the pursuit turned from the image to the system: I co-founded Pupil, a machine-learning and spatial-data company, and went on to Meta Reality Labs as Lead Product Designer on the Engagement and Growth team, working on discovery for Quest — what people saw when they put the headset on — where I began to see how systems shape attention itself.

A few years ago I started to suspect that the breakdowns around us — radicalisation, polarisation, outrage, eroding trust and attention spans — shared a common mechanism: digital systems feed on their own output until they consume themselves, and us with them. I spent the next two years taking the question apart, convinced there had to be something formal underneath — a claim specific enough to fail. That search became Loopzero — and its first real finding was not about the systems. It was that the instrument I built to study them was misreading its own data, and that I could not see it from the inside. Everything I do now starts there.

Every generation of the interface — feed, chat, agent — does more for you and shows you less about how it decides.

The instrument

Two years ago I built a detector for systems that degrade, and earlier this year I ran it. It worked — the numbers came back the way I’d predicted. But I’d pre-registered the analysis, which means I’d written down what would count as a real result before I saw any data, and timestamped it. Checked against that rule, most of my control group turned out to be unusable: they’d left the platform, and my labelling rule required a user to still be active to be marked as harmed. Everyone who quit had been recorded as fine.

The people most likely to have been hurt were the ones I had counted as healthy. I had built the thing to find them.

I rewrote it, ran it again under rules I couldn’t move, and my original claim failed. I published all of it.
