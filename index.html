Rule of thumb n. Any rough measure, trusted from practice rather than proof.

Every screen you touch is keeping score. The score reads you backwards.

I spent twenty years above the glass, designing what people see: moving image, cinematic VR, spatial computing, and eventually Engagement and Growth at Meta Reality Labs.

Then I went below it.

The problem

Rule of thumb

Here is what a system knows about your worst night. It knows you opened the app at 11:04, and 11:31, and 12:02, 12:20, 12:33, 12:41. It knows each session to the second and each scroll to the pixel. It knows the moment you stopped. By 2:47 it has several hundred events from you. On an engagement dashboard, that record can look like success.

Here is what it knows about what made your best hour good. Nothing. You put the phone face-down and sat with someone, or walked, or grieved. It knows when the events stopped. It doesn’t know what happened after them. Not a low score. No row.

An instrument can only count what reaches it.

English already had a phrase for a rough measure trusted from habit rather than proof. We never meant it literally. But what doesn’t reach an instrument doesn’t necessarily score badly.

Absence isn’t a low score. Sometimes it is no score at all.

That distinction is easy to miss, because the number still looks clean. An hour that left no evidence doesn’t arrive as a gap or a warning. It arrives as nothing, and the total is simply smaller. Absence from the instrument is not absence from the life.

There is a thumb on the scale. It isn’t pressing down. It’s the only thing the scale can feel.

The loop

Nothing stops at a reading. The reading becomes the instruction. The system serves more of what it saw; you respond, because what surrounds you is now built from the version of you it recorded; the next reading is taken under those conditions; it trains the next model; the model acts.

Each pass is made in an environment the last pass helped set. Nothing in the reading itself tells the system what it missed.

And the surface where you could watch this happen is thinning. A feed had a screen you could point at. A dashboard had column headers written in words. An agent can act beyond the surface where its judgements are visible. As more of the work is handed over, less of it happens where anyone can inspect it.

The interface thins. The loop remains.

The finding

I know this problem because I built one.

I spent two years building an instrument to detect harm before it became visible. It returned the result I wanted. Then a rule I had sealed against myself before opening the data showed that 3,292 of my 4,755 controls had left the platform — and my own definition required a person to still be present in order to count as harmed. Anyone who had gone could not appear as harmed under that rule, whatever had happened to them. I built the thing to find them, and I could not see the error from anywhere inside my own work.

I published the failure.

What that demonstrates is narrow, and it is about my own instrument: a rule that requires presence cannot register the cases that leave. Whether the same shape sits inside instruments I didn’t build is a question, not a result.

The world this builds, I call Thumb World.

After the digit that swipes, likes and trains. A world in the theme-park sense: an enclosure, engineered, mistaken for nature by the people inside it.

The investigation

I don’t know yet how far this pattern generalizes. That’s the investigation.

I’m writing Thumb World, a book about the rule of thumb and the people who live outside it — the scientists, teachers and traditions who train attention rather than capture it: contemplative practice, deep listening, art, and the room with no feed in it.

A better instrument isn’t enough if it is also the thing that decides whether it worked.

The check has to come from outside the loop.

A person, a sealed rule, an evaluator, a preregistration — whatever form it takes, it has to be something the instrument under test cannot certify for itself.

Finding out how common this is looks straightforward. Take published benchmarks, ask what happens to the cases they can’t score, and ask whether the reported number makes that visible. I haven’t found that done systematically. It is the next piece of work.

Over the next twelve months I’m talking with people who understand pieces of this better than I do: the people who build these instruments, the people who audit them, and the people who train attention without any instrument at all.

What would we build if success meant increasing a human capacity rather than increasing evidence of engagement?

I don’t have an answer. It is the question underneath the contemplative practice, the deep listening and the art — the difference between a success that needs you to keep interacting in order to be visible, and one that survives after the instrument is switched off.

If you understand a part of this better than I do, I’d like half an hour of your time.

The throughline

I came to this sideways. I studied art history at Northwestern, then found film through post-production. Cubism had shown me a problem that stayed with me: what happens to an image when there is no single fixed viewpoint?

At the Royal College of Art I trained as a designer, then carried the question into moving image, directing at Ridley Scott Associates. Virtual reality removed the edge of the frame altogether, and the image surrounded the observer. I made cinematic VR and founded a studio to build it.

Then the pursuit moved from the image to the system.

I co-founded Pupil, a machine-learning and spatial-data company, and later joined Meta Reality Labs, working on discovery for Quest — what people encountered when they put the headset on. There I worked on systems that learned from what people did and used it to shape what they saw next.

I left to look at what those systems couldn’t see, and found the same gap in an instrument I had built myself. The question moved once more: from the system to the measurement underneath it.
