Every screen you touch is keeping score — and the score reads you backwards.

A sleepless night of scrolling leaves a perfect trail: session length, returns, taps, reactions. The hour you put the phone down leaves nothing.

Capture leaves evidence of itself. Freedom, none.

Below the glass, machinery turns what it can see of your attention into instructions for what happens to you next. The result comes back through your interface, changes your behaviour, produces new signals, and the loop begins again.

The feed selected what appeared. Chat enters the exchange. Agents begin to act on its result. The machinery shaping attention is moving beyond the screen, into places where the interface itself begins to disappear.

I spent twenty years above the glass, designing what people see: moving image, cinematic VR, spatial computing, and eventually Engagement and Growth at Meta Reality Labs.

Then I went below it.

I followed the loop from the feed to the dashboard, from the dashboard to the benchmark, and finally to the story we tell ourselves about all three.

I’m interested in both ends of that loop: what happens to human attention as the systems shaping it become more intelligent and intimate — and what attention itself can become when it is deliberately trained rather than captured.

The investigation

The forms of attention easiest for machines to see are not necessarily the ones most valuable to a human life.

A system can measure a two-hour scroll in exquisite detail. An hour of stillness, concentration or chosen absence may leave no record at all.

That matters more as the interface changes.

A feed shapes what gets your attention.
Chat becomes a thinking partner.
Agents act on your behalf.

I want to understand what happens to human attention as that machinery moves beyond the screen — scientifically, culturally and experientially.

But I’m equally interested in the other end of the spectrum: what human attention can become when it is deliberately cultivated — through contemplative practice, deep listening, art and other forms of sustained attention.

Attention is not only something systems compete to capture. It is a human capacity with ranges we rarely design for: deep concentration, aesthetic absorption, contemplative experience, the sustained attention from which difficult thought and perception become possible.

The question isn’t only what our machines are doing to attention. It’s what forms of attention we want a human life to cultivate.

I’m assembling researchers, builders, artists and contemplatives who understand different pieces of this territory: how attention is shaped, how intelligent systems learn from it, how attention can be cultivated, and what our instruments fail to see.

The work will become a book and a wider body of research, writing and experience.

If you understand a part of this better than I do, I’d like half an hour of your time.

The throughline

I came to this sideways. I studied art history at Northwestern, then found film through post-production. Cubism had shown me a problem that stayed with me: what happens to an image when there is no single fixed viewpoint?

At the Royal College of Art I trained as a designer. Then I carried the question into moving image, directing at Ridley Scott Associates.

Virtual reality removed the edge of the frame. The image surrounded the observer. I made cinematic VR and founded a studio to build it.

Then the pursuit moved from the image to the system.

I co-founded Pupil, a machine-learning and spatial-data company, and later joined Meta Reality Labs as Lead Product Designer on Engagement and Growth, working on discovery for Quest — what people encountered when they put the headset on.

At Meta, I worked on systems that learned from what people did and used it to shape what they saw next.

I left to investigate what those systems couldn’t see. In the process, I discovered the same blind spot in an instrument I had built myself.

Absence wasn’t a low score. It was no score at all.

What happens when intelligent systems learn about us from what we do — but cannot learn from what we choose not to do?

Each new interface changes the question.

Feed. Headset. Chat. Agent.

The surface changes. The loop underneath becomes more intimate.
