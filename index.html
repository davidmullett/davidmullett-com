Rule of thumb n. Any rough measure, trusted from practice rather than proof.

Every screen you touch is keeping score. The score reads you backwards. And the part of you that would notice is the part it can’t see.

I spent twenty years above the glass, designing what people see: moving image, cinematic VR, spatial computing, and eventually Engagement and Growth at Meta Reality Labs.

Then I went below it.

The check has to come from outside the loop. That’s what I went looking for.

The problem

Rule of thumb

Here is what a system knows about your worst night. It knows you opened the app at 11:04, and 11:31, and 12:02, 12:20, 12:33, 12:41. It knows each session to the second and each scroll to the pixel. It knows the moment you stopped. By 2:47 it has several hundred events from you. On every dashboard that reads this record, you are a success.

Here is what it knows about your best hour. Nothing. You put the phone face-down and sat with someone, or walked, or grieved. There is no entry. Not a low score. No row.

Machines only count what touches them.

English already had a phrase for a rough measure trusted from habit rather than proof. We never meant it literally. But every system that decides something about you — what you’re shown, what you’re offered, whether you’re flagged, what a model trained on people like you will do on your behalf — runs on a measure that was never checked against the thing it claims to measure. Not hidden. Never run, because the number kept coming back clean.

The captured thumb scores as healthy. The thumb that put the phone down scores as absent. The sick read as well. The well read as gone.

Backwards. Both of them. Every time.

There is a thumb on the scale. It isn’t pressing down. It’s the only thing the scale can feel.

The loop

Nothing stops at a reading. The reading becomes the instruction. The system serves more of what it saw; you respond, because your surroundings are now built for the version of you it recorded; the next reading is sharper, in the same direction; it trains the next model; the model acts.

The machines don’t just read your story backwards. They write your next chapter from the backwards reading.

Run that for a year and the picture of you is wrong. Run it for a decade and the picture stops being wrong, because you’ve been shaped into it.

And the surface where you could once watch this happen is thinning. A feed had a screen you could point at. A dashboard had column headers written in words. An agent that acts for you has no surface at all. As the work is handed over, the judgements move underground — out of reach of anyone who might check them.

The era of no screens is the era of invisible scales.

The comfort

The old dystopias needed a villain. A boot, a Ministry, a Controller. This one needs none. Nobody drew the picture. Everyone who built these instruments wanted the right answer — the engineers, the researchers, the people who wrote the rules. I know, because I was one of them. Twice.

Huxley came closest: citizens who loved their servitude, a gramme better than a damn. But soma needed a pharmacist. What we built needs no one. It’s administered by your own thumb, and it produces not happiness but something quieter.

Everyone is fine.

Every number is good. The capacity to notice what’s missing was the first thing that stopped leaving a trace, so there’s nothing left to notice with. Not a boot. A pleasant hum. Not the end of the world — the end of the alarm.

The narrator

There’s one more instrument, and it’s the one you run on yourself.

Asked how you are, you consult the record — your memory, assembled from what can be told. And what can be told is what left a mark. The hours that actually made the week leave you nothing to say about them. So the story comes back coherent, and coherence feels like accuracy, and you say: I’m fine. I’m just relaxing. This is my choice.

There’s a hole in your field of vision where the nerve enters the eye. Your brain fills it so perfectly you have never once seen it. The loop fills in the missing person as fine the way the brain fills in the hole as scenery.

I know this from the inside. I spent two years building an instrument to detect harm before it became visible. It returned the result I wanted. Then a rule I had sealed against myself before opening the data showed that 3,292 of my 4,755 controls had left the platform — and my own definition required a person to still be present in order to count as harmed. Everyone who quit had been filed as healthy. I built the thing to find them, and I could not see the error from anywhere inside my own work. I published the failure.

The world this builds, I call Thumb World.

After the digit that swipes, likes and trains. A world in the theme-park sense: an enclosure, engineered, mistaken for nature by the people inside it.

The investigation

I’m writing Thumb World, a book about the rule of thumb and the people who live outside it — the scientists, teachers and traditions who train attention rather than capture it: contemplative practice, deep listening, art, and the room with no feed in it.

A better instrument isn’t the answer. A better instrument has a better builder with the same blind spot. What survives is a check that sits outside the loop — and territory the map can’t reach: the hour spent well, chosen for its own sake, because nothing will ever record it.

Nobody has measured how common the error is. The test is cheap: for every published benchmark, what happens to the cases it can’t score, and does the number say so. That census is the next piece of work.

Over the next twelve months I’m interviewing the people who understand pieces of this better than I do: the researchers who build these instruments, the people who study them, and the people who train attention without any of them.

If you understand a part of this better than I do, I’d like half an hour of your time.

The throughline

I came to this sideways. I studied art history at Northwestern, then found film through post-production. Cubism had shown me a problem that stayed with me: what happens to an image when there is no single fixed viewpoint?

At the Royal College of Art I trained as a designer. Then I carried the question into moving image, directing at Ridley Scott Associates.

Virtual reality removed the edge of the frame. The image surrounded the observer. I made cinematic VR and founded a studio to build it.

Then the pursuit moved from the image to the system.

I co-founded Pupil, a machine-learning and spatial-data company, and later joined Meta Reality Labs as Lead Product Designer on Engagement and Growth, working on discovery for Quest — what people encountered when they put the headset on.

At Meta, I worked on systems that learned from what people did and used it to shape what they saw next.

I left to investigate what those systems couldn’t see. In the process, I discovered the same blind spot in an instrument I had built myself.

Absence wasn’t a low score. It was no score at all.

What happens when intelligent systems learn about us from what we do, but cannot learn from what we choose not to do?

Each new interface changes the question.

Feed. Headset. Chat. Agent. Post-screen.

The interface thins. The loop does not, and it decides more of what reaches you.
