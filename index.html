Earlier this year I built a test for a theory I believed in, and the test said the theory worked. Then a safeguard I’d locked in before running it showed the test itself was broken — its fine print was quietly filing the worst failures as successes. I fixed the test, ran it again under rules I couldn’t move, and my theory failed. I published that, in full.

Most teams never find out whether their test is broken. That’s the work.

What I do

  • Benchmark validity audit · 2–3 weeks — Your evals, harm metrics, and success numbers — audited for what they structurally can’t see. Which failures can your measurement never record? How many of your “good” outcomes are that? You get an evidence packet, and a repair if there’s a problem.
  • Design sprint · 2–6 weeks — Trust surfaces for agents — consent, receipts, interruption, handoff to a human. AI-native product definition. Discovery, ranking, browse. Redesigns that move a product off a metric that lies.
  • The seat · 1–2 days a week — Fractional design principal: direction, critique, hiring, and the standing argument with the metrics.

I don’t do production design. I decide what gets built, define how it behaves, and make sure the numbers you steer by are honest.

The throughline

I came to this sideways.

I studied art history at Northwestern, then found film through post-production. Cubism had shown me the goal: an image that refuses a single fixed viewpoint. The Royal College of Art trained me as a designer; the pursuit carried on, bringing that eye to the moving image as a director at Ridley Scott Associates.

Virtual reality finally exploded the frame — not just past the screen but all the way around you, embodying what I’d spent years circling. I made cinematic VR and founded a studio to build it. Then the pursuit turned from the image to the system: I co-founded Pupil, a machine-learning and spatial-data company, and went on to Meta Reality Labs as Lead Product Designer for Engagement & Growth, building immersive digital products at scale — where I began to see how systems shape attention itself.

A few years ago I started to suspect that the breakdowns around us — radicalisation, polarisation, outrage, eroding trust and attention spans — shared a common mechanism: digital systems feed on their own output until they consume themselves, and us with them. I spent the next two years taking the question apart, convinced there had to be something formal underneath — a claim specific enough to fail. That search became Loopzero — and its first real finding was not about the systems. It was that the instrument I built to study them was misreading its own data, and that I could not see it from the inside. Everything I do now starts there.
